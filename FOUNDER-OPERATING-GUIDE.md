# Founder Slipwell Operating Guide

## Purpose

This guide describes how the founder should use Slipwell as the first dogfooding user. It is a personal operating guide, a repeatable product-demo story, and a source of realistic workflows for product validation.

It does not redefine Slipwell's primary customer profile. The founder is one concrete example of an independent professional balancing recurring clients, finite projects, personal commitments, and ventures without an operations team.

Do not enter employer-confidential information, credentials, private client metrics, lead data, or other sensitive contents while Slipwell remains a validation prototype. Use anonymized or low-sensitivity information in demonstrations.

## The founder story

> I am a software engineer with a full-time role. Outside work, I manage paid social advertising for an online education business, maintain websites for local companies, build Slipwell, develop an AI consultancy, and explore a clothing brand. Each responsibility has a different cadence. Some commitments repeat every day or month, some arrive unexpectedly, and my own ventures have no client asking why they have not moved. Slipwell helps me capture commitments when they appear, organize them without breaking focus, and deliberately return attention to important work before it becomes a problem.

The underlying problem is not an inability to create tasks. It is context fragmentation:

- The full-time role has structured external systems and immediate expectations.
- Client work creates revenue, deadlines, and unpredictable requests.
- Slipwell, the AI consultancy, and the clothing brand depend on self-directed attention.
- Personal commitments compete with all of the above.
- Notes, messages, calendars, task lists, and memory do not provide one trustworthy attention layer.

Slipwell's job is not to operate these businesses automatically. Its job is to preserve commitments, reduce the cost of switching contexts, and show what needs deliberate attention.

## Recommended configuration

### Domains

| Domain | What belongs there | What does not belong there |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Career | Personal commitments related to the full-time software engineering role | Employer source code, secrets, confidential customer data, or the employer's complete project system |
| Client Services | Advertising and website-client work | Slipwell, AI consultancy, clothing-brand, or employer work |
| Slipwell | Product validation, founder alpha, product development, and launch work | Unrelated AI-consultancy delivery |
| AI Consultancy | Offer validation, agent demonstrations, prospecting, sales, and delivery | Slipwell product work unless Slipwell is explicitly the subject |
| Clothing Brand | Brand validation, design, sourcing, content, and launch work | Generic personal shopping or unrelated creative notes |
| Personal | Health, household, relationships, appointments, and administration | Client deliverables |

Keep this list stable. Create finite projects underneath the domains instead of creating a new domain for every idea.

### People

Create lightweight records for important client and prospect contacts. Record only useful professional context:

- Role and organization
- Relationship context
- Relevant projects or retainers
- Important decisions or requests
- Follow-up tasks
- Sourced interaction notes

Do not copy entire private conversations into a person record without a clear reason.

## Workstream setup

### 1. Career

Use Slipwell as a private personal execution layer, not as a replacement for Jira, GitHub, Slack, or employer systems.

Examples:

- Prepare examples for an architecture discussion.
- Follow up on a decision after a meeting.
- Research a technical approach.
- Complete an administrative deadline.
- Record a professional-development idea.

Suggested Slipping cadence: only for personally owned projects that should receive attention every 7 to 14 days. Do not duplicate every assigned engineering ticket.

### 2. Advertising client

Model the advertising engagement as a monthly retainer with recurring deliverables, one-off tasks, carry-forward behavior, and preserved history.

#### Daily expected work

- Check spend and campaign delivery.
- Check leads and cost per lead.
- Look for unusual performance changes.
- Record the observation that matters for the next update.
- Send the agreed daily report.

#### Weekly expected work

- Prepare the weekly performance summary.
- Compare performance with the previous week.
- Explain meaningful changes.
- Recommend the next experiment or adjustment.
- Request missing sales or lead-quality feedback.
- Discuss the next move with the client.

#### Monthly expected work

- Prepare the monthly analytics report.
- Compare with the previous month and relevant baseline.
- Review lead quality and sales feedback.
- Evaluate creative, audience, offer, landing-page, and tracking performance.
- Record decisions for the next monthly cycle.

Daily reporting is professional client work, not a routine. Keep it connected to the retainer so it can appear in the current cycle, history, and Slipping calculations.

Example capture:

> Education ads client: cost per lead increased today. Check whether the new creative caused it and include the conclusion in Friday's report.

Expected proposal:

- Investigation task linked to the current retainer cycle
- Note or report input linked to the weekly update
- Appropriate date and priority
- Visible routing explanation and review state

### 3. Local-business websites

Keep each client as a person or client context. Create a finite project when a defined batch of requested changes arrives.

Example project:

**Add a new HVAC service to the company website**

- Confirm scope and intended audience.
- Request final service copy.
- Request images and project examples.
- Design and build the service page.
- Update navigation and internal links.
- Test desktop and mobile behavior.
- Send a preview for approval.
- Apply revisions.
- Publish and confirm completion.

Complete the project after the requested outcome is delivered. Do not leave a permanent active project waiting for a request that may not arrive for months.

Use a retainer only if a website client has a continuing maintenance agreement with recurring expected work. Sporadic requests alone do not make the relationship a retainer.

Example capture:

> HVAC client has a new heat-pump service. Ask for the copy and photos, estimate the work, and update the website after approval.

### 4. Slipwell

Slipwell is a durable domain. Work inside it should be expressed as finite outcomes.

Recommended current project:

**Recruit and learn from the first 10 concierge-alpha users**

- Finish the founder dogfooding setup.
- Record three truthful product demonstrations.
- Publish the founder story.
- Interview qualified prospects.
- Onboard the strongest matches.
- Observe real captures and corrections.
- Record repeated friction and value moments.
- Decide what to build next from evidence.

Suggested Slipping cadence: 7 days.

Product activity is not automatically validation activity. A week of code changes should not reset the attention cadence of an alpha-recruitment project unless it materially helps recruit or learn from users.

### 5. AI consultancy

Create finite projects instead of one permanent project called `Build AI consultancy`.

Recommended first project:

**Validate an AI automation offer with 10 local businesses**

- Select one or two candidate industries.
- Write the problem hypothesis.
- Interview businesses about the current workflow.
- Identify one expensive or repetitive problem.
- Build a narrow demonstration.
- Test the offer and price.
- Record objections and buying signals.
- Decide whether to proceed, revise, or stop.

Later projects may include launching the consultancy site, acquiring the first client, delivering the first engagement, and publishing a case study.

Suggested Slipping cadence: 7 days while actively validating, or paused when Slipwell is the deliberate priority.

### 6. Clothing brand

Keep only one active outcome until demand is validated.

Recommended first project:

**Validate the first clothing concept before purchasing inventory**

- Define the intended customer.
- Establish the visual direction.
- Create the first concept set.
- Source and compare samples.
- Calculate production cost and target margin.
- Create realistic mockups.
- Show the concepts to target customers.
- Decide whether to produce a small release.

Suggested Slipping cadence: 14 days while active. Pause it explicitly if another venture deserves the current focus.

### 7. Personal life and routines

Use routines only for repeated personal behavior:

- Morning planning
- Exercise
- Reading
- Evening shutdown
- Weekly review

Client reports, publishing commitments, prospecting, and product launches are tasks or project work, not routines.

## Capture rules

Capture first when a thought, promise, request, decision, or useful piece of context appears. Do not stop deep work to design the perfect structure.

Useful capture patterns:

- `Client + observation + intended action + timing`
- `Person + interaction + promised follow-up`
- `Project + decision + next action`
- `Idea + why it matters`, without inventing a deadline
- `Personal commitment + actual date`, when one exists

Examples:

- `Ads client: investigate today's lead drop and include the conclusion in Friday's report.`
- `HVAC client wants a new service page. Ask for copy and photos before estimating.`
- `Slipwell post idea: my clients have deadlines, but my own business only has intentions.`
- `AI consultancy: interview dental offices about missed-call follow-up.`
- `Clothing brand: compare heavyweight blank samples before choosing a supplier.`
- `Book a dentist appointment next week.`

Review proposals when a date, person, destination, recurrence, or relationship is ambiguous. Keep auto-file review-first until routing has earned trust.

## Operating rhythm

### Morning, five minutes

1. Open Today.
2. Check calendar context and real deadlines.
3. Review the highest-value Slipping signals.
4. Confirm unresolved captures.
5. Choose a realistic Top Three.

A Top Three does not need one item from every domain. It represents what can realistically receive attention today.

### During the day

Capture commitments without reorganizing the whole system. Return to the current activity. Review ambiguous proposals during a natural break.

### Evening, five minutes

1. Review unresolved captures.
2. Complete, reschedule, or intentionally defer open commitments.
3. Record meaningful progress or a waiting state.
4. Check tomorrow's calendar.
5. Let the Top Three reset rather than silently carrying it forward.

### Weekly, 20 to 30 minutes

For each domain, ask:

- What materially moved?
- What is waiting on somebody else?
- What became Slipping?
- Which venture received no attention?
- What should be paused?
- What is the smallest next action?
- What should not remain an active commitment?

### Monthly

- Review and close the advertising retainer cycle.
- Inspect completed and carried-forward work.
- Review client-service capacity.
- Decide which venture deserves active focus.
- Pause projects that are not genuinely active.
- Review important relationship follow-ups.
- Export important data when the production feature is available.

## First-person product demo story

Use this narrative when introducing the product:

> I am a software engineer with a full-time job, but that is only one part of my week. I also manage advertising for a client, maintain websites for local businesses, build Slipwell, develop an AI consultancy, and explore a clothing brand. Every responsibility behaves differently. Advertising reports repeat. Website requests arrive randomly. My own businesses have no external deadline. I built Slipwell because urgent work always had a way to find me, while important work could quietly disappear.

## Repeatable demo flow

Keep the main demonstration under five minutes. Use synthetic names and numbers.

### Scene 1: Show the problem

Briefly show or describe the disconnected inputs: a client message, a performance observation, an idea, and an unfinished venture. Do not spend time listing every feature.

Say:

> These commitments arrive while I am already doing something else. Organizing them immediately costs focus, but trusting myself to remember them is unreliable.

### Scene 2: Capture a multi-intent client observation

Enter:

> For the ads client, investigate today's lead-cost increase and include the conclusion in Friday's update.

Show:

- Original source preservation
- Proposed task and note or report input
- Retainer and date relationships
- Routing explanation
- Review, correction, confirmation, and undo controls

Say:

> Slipwell proposes. I remain in control of what becomes part of my system.

### Scene 3: Show recurring client work

Open the advertising retainer and current monthly cycle. Show daily, weekly, and monthly expectations, one-off work, history, and carry-forward behavior.

Say:

> A retainer is not just one recurring task. It has a current cycle, expected work, new requests, unfinished obligations, and history.

### Scene 4: Capture an unpredictable website request

Enter:

> HVAC client wants a new heat-pump service page. Ask for copy and photos, estimate it, and publish after approval.

Show how a finite project and its next actions differ from the ongoing advertising retainer.

### Scene 5: Show Today

Show calendar context, tasks, routines, recent captures, Slipping, and the user-selected Top Three.

Say:

> Slipwell can suggest attention, but I choose the final three priorities. It is an attention system, not an automatic scheduler.

### Scene 6: Show Slipping

Open a synthetic signal for the AI consultancy:

> No meaningful activity for 10 days. Expected cadence: 7 days.

Show the available actions: add a next action, record progress, defer, change cadence, pause, archive, or dismiss.

Say:

> Nothing here is overdue. That is the point. My clients remind me what they need. Slipwell helps me notice when my own business is being displaced.

### Scene 7: Close with the outcome

Say:

> Slipwell does not do my work for me. It lets me capture without breaking focus, run recurring and finite work correctly, and stop carrying every open commitment in my head.

## Honest demo boundaries

- Demonstrate only behavior that works in the current build.
- Label planned PRD behavior as a concept or upcoming workflow.
- Do not imply that calendar sync, production notifications, billing, deletion, or other unfinished gates are complete.
- Use synthetic client names, performance metrics, contacts, and capture contents.
- Do not describe the current prototype as the finished commercial MVP.

## Personal success scorecard

Review this each week:

- Did I capture on at least two separate days?
- Did capture reduce an interruption or prevent an open loop?
- Were proposals structurally correct?
- Did I correct any unsafe or confusing routing?
- Did the advertising retainer reduce checklist reconstruction?
- Did a Slipping signal cause useful action or intentional deferment?
- Did Today help me choose rather than merely display work?
- Did I intentionally pause a venture instead of pretending everything was active?
- Did Slipwell prevent or clarify a meaningful commitment?

The strongest founder evidence is not time spent in the app. It is resolved commitments, reduced mental load, useful correction data, and specific moments when something important did not slip.
