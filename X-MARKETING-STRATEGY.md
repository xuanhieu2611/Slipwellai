# Slipwell X Marketing Strategy

## Purpose

This document is the working strategy for founder-led marketing and validation on X. Use it to decide what to post, what media to include, how often to publish, and how to invite qualified people into the Slipwell alpha without making the account feel like a stream of advertisements.

The immediate objective is not maximum reach. It is qualified recognition, conversation, interviews, and repeated product use from people who match Slipwell's behavioral customer profile.

## Start here: ordered founder-validation campaign

This is the section to follow when deciding what to publish next. Start with Post 1 and continue in numerical order. The days are sequence markers, not fixed calendar dates. If a post is delayed, publish it when it is ready and continue with the next number. Do not restart the campaign because a day was missed.

This is not a public product launch. It is a two-week founder-validation campaign designed to produce qualified conversations, problem interviews, and a small concierge alpha. Do not add a pricing link or ask people to create an account during this sequence.

### Campaign rules

- Publish four or five times per week, not several times per day.
- Keep at least one day between product demonstrations when practical.
- Stay available for 30 to 45 minutes after publishing to answer replies.
- Do not add hashtags to these posts.
- Use only synthetic or explicitly approved information in product media.
- Use the same synthetic example throughout the campaign so the story feels connected.
- Do not post a feature that is mocked, unreliable, or unavailable in the demonstrated flow.
- Do not change the wording to call Slipwell an `AI productivity app`, `life operating system`, or `better to-do list`.
- Do not introduce bring-your-own-key, self-hosting, model selection, or implementation tutorials during this campaign. Those subjects attract a developer audience and distract from customer validation.

Recommended recurring synthetic example:

> Rivera Studio wants a heat-pump service page. Ask Maya for the copy and photos before estimating. Follow up Friday.

### Before Post 1

Complete this setup once:

1. Use a clear photograph of the founder as the profile photo.
2. Use this profile bio, adjusting `junior` only if it accurately reflects the founder's current title:

   > Junior software engineer balancing a full-time role, client work, and products of my own. Building Slipwell so nothing important slips through.

3. Allow replies and direct messages from potential interview participants.
4. Prepare one real founder photograph and one 15-second capture demonstration.
5. Remove employer information, client information, browser tabs, notifications, email addresses, and real capture contents from every asset.

Do not wait for a polished brand banner, launch page, or promotional video. The founder story and the working prototype are enough for this campaign.

Crop closely enough that product text is readable on a phone. Use a large cursor, remove loading pauses, and burn the overlay captions into the video. Do not use spoken narration unless it adds meaning that the on-screen sequence cannot communicate.

### Post 5: Honest weekly learning

**When**

Publish after the first four posts have had at least 24 hours to collect replies. Use only real counts and truthful observations.

**Copy template**

> My first week sharing Slipwell:

> - [X] useful conversations

>

Replace every placeholder before posting. If there were no qualified conversations, say so plainly rather than publishing invented or misleading evidence.

**Low-response alternative**

>
> But it gave me [X] real conversations about how people handle commitments across clients and personal projects.
>

**Media**

An image is optional. If used, create a square, count-only scorecard containing the four real numbers. Do not include names, quotes attributed to identifiable people, client details, screenshots of messages, or private capture contents. If a scorecard would contain only zeros, use text only.

### Post 6: Slipping distinction

**When**

Publish after one or two days without a product demonstration.

**Copy**

> This project was not overdue.
>
> It had simply received no meaningful attention for 11 days.
>
> Deadlines show what is late.
> Slipping shows what is quietly being displaced.
>
> I am testing whether that distinction is useful or just more noise. Which does it feel like to you?

**Media: required screenshot**

Use one tightly cropped screenshot of a synthetic Slipping signal:

- Project name: `Validate the consultancy offer`.
- Explanation: `No meaningful attention for 11 days`.
- Expected cadence: `Every 7 days`.
- Include the actions `Add next action`, `Defer`, and `Pause` if they are available in the demonstrated screen.
- Add no more than two annotations. Point once to `11 days` and once to the expected cadence.
- Do not show the entire Today dashboard or unrelated tasks.

**After publishing**

Classify substantive responses as `useful distinction`, `sounds stressful`, `already solved`, or `unclear`. Ask people who dislike the idea what would make the signal feel respectful rather than judgmental.

### Post 7: Visible AI correction

**When**

Publish one day after Post 6 or on the next normal publishing day.

**Copy**

> I gave Slipwell an ambiguous capture today and it chose the wrong project.
>
> The original stayed intact. I corrected the proposal before filing it.
>
> I do not think trustworthy AI means pretending mistakes disappear.
>
> It means making them visible, correctable, and reversible.

Only use `today` if the founder actually performed the test that day. Otherwise replace the first sentence with `I tested Slipwell with an ambiguous capture and it chose the wrong project.`

**Media: required video**

Create a 15-second correction video:

1. Show the ambiguous synthetic capture.
2. Show the incorrect proposed destination.
3. Change the destination.
4. Confirm the proposal.
5. End on the preserved original capture with the overlay `AI proposes. I decide.`

Do not describe a staged ambiguity as an accidental real-world failure. The purpose is to show the recovery design honestly, not manufacture drama.

### Post 8: Retainer distinction

**When**

Publish approximately two days after Post 7.

**Copy**

> Recurring client work is not one recurring task.
>
> Every month has expected work, new requests, unfinished items, decisions, and history.
>
> I built retainers into Slipwell because I was tired of rebuilding the same client checklist while losing the context from last month.

**Media: required video**

1. Show the August `Rivera Studio advertising` cycle.
2. Leave `Update landing-page experiment` open.

4. Show the item carried forward with its link to August.
5. End with the overlay `New cycle. History preserved.`

**After publishing**

Ask relevant respondents:

> How do you rebuild or carry forward recurring client work today?

Prioritize conversations with people who manage monthly deliverables, retainers, reporting cycles, or repeated client operations.

### Post 9: Concierge-alpha invitation

**When**

Publish after the previous posts have established the founder, problem, capture proof, and professional workflow. Do not move this invitation earlier merely because an early post receives many impressions.

**Copy**

> Looking for 10 people balancing a main job or business, recurring clients, and something of their own.
>
> Slipwell is an early alpha, not a finished productivity platform.
>
> I want to learn your workflow and test one use case with you. DM me `slip` if this sounds like your week.

**Media: required founder video**

Record a direct-to-camera video in the same setting as the Post 1 photograph. Use this spoken script:

> Do you balance a primary job or business, client work, and a project of your own? I'm testing Slipwell with ten people who live that week. It is an early alpha, not a polished launch. DM me `slip` if that sounds like you.

Keep the video close to 15 seconds. Add accurate burned-in captions. End on the founder, not a logo animation.

**After publishing**

Send qualified respondents this message:

> Thanks for replying to my Slipwell post. I'm researching how people balance primary work, clients, and projects of their own. Would you be open to a 20-minute call about your current workflow? This is research first. You do not need to prepare or migrate anything.

Do not promise alpha access to everyone. First confirm that the person experiences the target behavior and is willing to show or describe a real workflow.

### What to do after Post 9

Do not immediately repeat the same campaign. Review the evidence first:

1. Count qualified replies, DMs, interviews, and alpha invitations accepted.
2. Group the language people used around capture, recurring client work, displaced projects, and AI trust.
3. Identify which of capture, retainers, or Slipping produced the strongest qualified recognition.
4. Record whether people described a recent failure, not merely general interest.
5. Continue with the four-week thematic campaign below, emphasizing the strongest validated problem.
6. Repeat this founder sequence only when there is new evidence, a meaningful product outcome, or a genuinely new audience entering the account.

### Reply guide for predictable criticism

If someone says `This is another to-do app`:

> Fair concern. There are already excellent task managers. I'm testing a narrower problem: recurring client work and important projects that have no external deadline. If that distinction is not useful, I want to learn that early.

If someone says `This is AI slop`:

> That is exactly the risk I'm trying to avoid. The AI only proposes structure. The original capture stays intact, and every result can be corrected or undone. I'm testing whether that is genuinely useful before treating this as a finished product.

If someone says `I could build this in Notion`:

> You probably could. I'm building for people who do not want to design and maintain the system themselves. I'm interested in where your Notion setup works well and where it still requires manual upkeep.

Respond once with curiosity. Do not argue with insults or turn criticism into a defensive thread.

## Audience

Speak to independent professionals who recognize most of this pattern:

- They have a full-time role, primary business, or demanding professional responsibility.
- They also manage recurring clients or unpredictable client work.
- They are trying to build something of their own.
- They change context frequently and have no operations team.
- Their current system is spread across messages, notes, calendars, task tools, and memory.
- Urgent external work crowds out important self-directed work.

Do not broaden the message to every busy person, every developer, or everyone with side projects. Lead with a narrow lived problem and allow adjacent people to recognize themselves.

## Positioning

### Core tension

> My clients have deadlines. My own business only has intentions.

### Product promise

> Capture anything. Nothing important slips through.

### Founder explanation

> I built Slipwell because I have a full-time engineering role, recurring client obligations, unpredictable website work, and several ventures of my own. It helps me capture commitments without breaking focus and notice important work before neglect becomes a problem.

### What not to lead with

- AI productivity app
- All-in-one life operating system
- A list of every feature
- Automated scheduling
- Claims that AI runs the user's business or life
- Generic promises to make everyone more productive

## Campaign principle

Use three actions:

1. **Document** the honest operating problem.
2. **Demonstrate** one real product outcome.
3. **Invite** similar people into a conversation or experiment.

Most posts should be useful or recognizable without requiring the reader to sign up.

## Four content pillars

### Pillar 1: The multi-hat operator

Share recognizable moments from balancing employment, clients, personal life, and ventures.

Good subjects:

- External urgency versus internal importance
- Context switching
- Random client requests
- Rebuilding recurring checklists
- Side projects quietly disappearing
- The mental cost of remembering where everything belongs

Best formats:

- Text-only observation
- Short personal story
- Simple photo of the real working context with no sensitive information
- Minimal diagram showing competing contexts

Example hooks:

- `My clients have deadlines. My own business only has intentions.`
- `Every project matters. Only two have someone asking me for an update.`
- `I am not short on task managers. I am short on confidence that I will see the right task again.`

### Pillar 2: Real Slipwell workflows

Demonstrate one narrow workflow at a time.

Good subjects:

- A raw sentence becoming linked records
- A client request becoming a finite project
- Retainer cycle generation and carry-forward
- An explained Slipping signal
- Choosing a Top Three
- Correcting an AI proposal
- Recovering from a failed interpretation

Best formats:

- Short screen recording
- Before-and-after image
- Annotated screenshot
- Small sequence of two or three screenshots

Always show the original problem before showing the interface.

### Pillar 3: Product decisions and lessons

Explain why Slipwell behaves the way it does.

Good subjects:

- Routines are not tasks.
- Stale is different from overdue.
- A retainer is not a recurring project.
- AI proposes and the user remains in control.
- Original captures must be preserved.
- Today should help users choose, not become an analytics dashboard.
- Product development is not automatically product validation.

Best formats:

- Text-only opinion
- Diagram or comparison image
- Product screenshot followed by the reasoning
- Short talking-head explanation with a screen insert

Translate technical decisions into customer consequences. Avoid attracting only developers with implementation details.

### Pillar 4: Honest founder progress

Share evidence, learning, corrections, and uncertainty.

Good subjects:

- A commitment Slipwell helped recover
- A proposal Slipwell routed incorrectly
- What confused an alpha user
- A feature deliberately postponed
- Interview patterns
- Weekly capture and resolved-commitment results
- A positioning assumption that changed

Best formats:

- Text reflection
- Sanitized weekly scorecard image
- Short video recap
- Screenshot with private contents removed

Do not turn every coding session into a progress post. Report what changed for the user or what was learned.

## Weekly posting rhythm

Four or five posts per week is enough. Consistency and conversation matter more than filling every day.

| Day | Post type | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monday | Multi-hat problem | A personal observation about external urgency crowding out a venture |
| Tuesday | Workflow demo | One capture, retainer, Today, or Slipping interaction |
| Wednesday | Product decision | Why Slipwell models something differently |
| Thursday | Validation question | Ask readers how they currently handle the demonstrated problem |
| Friday | Founder outcome | What Slipwell changed, failed to change, or taught you that week |
| Weekend | Optional longer reflection | A thread, build note, interview synthesis, or weekly review |

Do not place a signup call to action on every post. Rotate among:

- No call to action
- `How do you handle this today?`
- `What tool does this live in for you?`
- `Reply if this sounds like your week.`
- `DM me if you want to join the 10-person alpha.`

## Media decision guide

### Use text only when

- The thought is personal, sharp, or emotionally recognizable.
- The post asks a direct workflow question.
- The product principle can be understood without the interface.
- A visual would merely repeat the text.

Text-only posts are appropriate for founder observations and validation questions.

### Use one image when

- A static state is the proof.
- A before-and-after comparison is immediately legible.
- A Slipping explanation, retainer cycle, or Today arrangement can be understood without interaction.
- You want to share a sanitized weekly scorecard.

Crop tightly around the relevant area. Remove client names, capture contents, email addresses, metrics, and unrelated interface clutter. Add one or two annotations at most.

### Use a video when

- The value comes from transformation or sequence.
- You are showing capture to proposal to confirmation.
- You are demonstrating correction, undo, carry-forward, or a Slipping action.
- A viewer needs to see how little effort the workflow requires.

Recommended structure for a short demo:

1. First two seconds: state the problem in text on screen.
2. Next 10 seconds: show the interaction without unnecessary cursor movement.
3. Final three seconds: show the outcome and one sentence of meaning.

Use captions. Zoom or crop so text is readable on a phone. Remove dead time, loading waits, private browser tabs, notifications, and personal information.

### Use a thread when

- The story needs a clear beginning, tension, decision, and result.
- You are launching the founder narrative.
- You are sharing a substantial product lesson or validation result.

Do not split a thought into a thread merely to create more posts.

## Four-week campaign

Use this thematic campaign only after completing the ordered founder-validation campaign in `Start here`. It describes what to emphasize next, not a second sequence to run at the same time.

### Week 1: Make the problem recognizable

Goal: establish the founder story and find people who share the operating pattern.

- Publish the founder-story launch thread.
- Publish one text-only observation about external urgency.
- Ask where unexpected commitments currently go.
- Reply thoughtfully to every relevant response.

### Week 2: Prove capture value

Goal: demonstrate that one unstructured sentence can become trustworthy structure.

- Publish a short capture-to-review video.
- Show one correction or ambiguity instead of presenting AI as perfect.
- Ask what prevents people from trusting automatic organization.
- Invite a small number of qualified respondents to an interview.

### Week 3: Prove the professional wedge

Goal: differentiate Slipwell from a generic task manager.

- Demonstrate a retainer cycle and carry-forward.
- Explain why sporadic website work is a finite project rather than a permanent retainer.
- Ask recurring-client professionals how they rebuild monthly work today.

### Week 4: Prove the attention wedge

Goal: make Slipping understandable and useful.

- Show a non-overdue but neglected project.
- Explain meaningful attention and intentional deferment.
- Share one real founder outcome.
- Invite the best-fit people into the concierge alpha.

Repeat the themes with new evidence instead of inventing new positioning every month.

## Conversation and conversion path

```text
Founder story
  -> recognizable workflow
  -> reply or DM
  -> 20-minute problem interview
  -> concierge setup
  -> one week of real use
  -> feedback interview
  -> founding alpha member
```

Do not send every interested reader directly to a pricing page. Learn about the current behavior before teaching the product.

Suggested interview prompts:

- Tell me about the last unexpected commitment you nearly forgot.
- Where did you put it when it arrived?
- How do recurring client obligations work today?
- What important work receives no external pressure?

## Validation metrics

Prioritize:

- Qualified replies
- Interviews booked and completed
- People willing to show their current workflow
- Concierge-alpha invitations accepted
- Captures completed on at least two separate days
- Structural acceptance of proposals
- Retainer cycles used in real work
- Slipping signals producing completion, a next action, deferment, cadence change, or intentional dismissal
- One-week and six-week retained usage
- Willingness to pay at least $12 to $15 per month

Do not use impressions, likes, or follower growth as the primary validation result. Record acquisition source so qualified target users can be distinguished from broad social attention.

## Publishing checklist

Before posting:

- Does the post communicate one idea?
- Is the customer problem understandable before the product appears?
- Is the demonstrated behavior actually implemented?
- Are planned features clearly labeled?
- Are all names, metrics, messages, emails, calendars, and captures synthetic or approved for publication?
- Is text readable on a phone?
- Does the video remove loading waits and dead time?
- Does the post avoid exaggerated productivity claims?
- Is the call to action appropriate, or does the post work better without one?

After posting:

- Reply thoughtfully to relevant responses.
- Ask about current behavior before explaining more features.
- Record qualified interview leads.
- Save recurring language used by prospects.
- Do not treat positive comments as willingness to use or pay.

## Guardrails

- Do not expose real client names or advertising performance without explicit permission.
- Do not share employer-confidential information.
- Do not claim Slipwell is a finished public MVP.
- Do not demonstrate mocked behavior as if it is live.
- Do not manufacture testimonials, usage, or urgency.
- Do not make every post promotional.
- Do not change the product roadmap because one post receives engagement.
- Do not let broad developer attention dilute the independent-professional ICP.

The desired tone is honest, calm, specific, and useful:

> This is the problem I experience. This is what I built. This is what happened when I used it. Does your week look anything like mine?
